# Italian Food

Italian cooking is regional to a degree that surprises people. The dishes in this chapter come from Naples, Piedmont, Sicily, Alto Adige, Veneto, Emilia, Valle d'Aosta, Abruzzo, and Puglia, and several of them would be unrecognisable to a cook two provinces away.

The recurring principle is restraint. Fewer ingredients, better ingredients, less interference. If a recipe here has six components, six is the number it needs.

Two entries are cured products rather than cooked dishes. Prosciutto di San Daniele is a protected ham you buy rather than make, so I've written how to serve it properly. Guanciale you genuinely can cure at home, and I've given the full method.

---

## 1. Pizza Napoletana

Two-day dough, blistered edge, soupy centre. Home ovens can't reach 450°C, so the fix is a very hot steel or stone plus the grill element.

**Makes 4 pizzas | Prep 30 min plus 24 hr | Cook 8 min each**

**Ingredients**

*Dough*
- 600 g Italian 00 flour, W280 or higher
- 390 ml water (65% hydration)
- 18 g fine sea salt
- 0.6 g fresh yeast, or a scant ¼ tsp instant

*Topping per pizza*
- 80 g San Marzano tomatoes, crushed by hand, salted, nothing else added
- 70 g fior di latte or buffalo mozzarella, torn and drained
- Fresh basil leaves
- Extra virgin olive oil

**Method**

1. Dissolve the salt in the water, then add about a tenth of the flour and whisk. Crumble in the yeast, then add the rest of the flour.
2. Mix to a rough dough, rest 20 minutes, then knead 10 minutes until smooth and elastic. Rest covered for 2 hours at room temperature.
3. Divide into four 245 g balls, shaping each tightly with a closed seam underneath.
4. Store in a lidded container and refrigerate 24 hours, then bring back to room temperature for 3 hours before baking. They should look domed and relaxed.
5. Heat your oven as hot as it goes with a steel or stone on the upper rack for at least 45 minutes. Switch to the grill element 10 minutes before baking.
6. Stretch a ball on a floured surface using your fingertips from the centre outward, always leaving the outer 2 cm untouched. That untouched ring becomes the cornicione. Never use a rolling pin.
7. Top quickly: tomato first, then cheese, then a drizzle of oil. Bake 5 to 7 minutes until the crust is puffed and spotted. Add basil as it comes out.

**Cook's note:** Drain the mozzarella on paper towels for an hour beforehand. Wet cheese is the main cause of a soggy centre at home.

## 2. Prosciutto di San Daniele

A protected ham from Friuli, cured with nothing but sea salt for at least 13 months. You can't make it, and you shouldn't try. What you can do is serve it properly, which most people don't.

**Serves 6 as antipasto | Prep 20 min | Cook 10 min**

**Ingredients**
- 200 g Prosciutto di San Daniele, sliced to order
- 1 ripe cantaloupe or 4 fresh figs
- 150 g burrata or stracciatella
- Grissini, or thin focaccia
- Extra virgin olive oil
- Cracked black pepper
- A few leaves of rocket

**Method**

1. Buy it sliced fresh from the counter, not vacuum packed. Ask for it cut thin enough to see light through, but not so thin it shreds.
2. Ask them to layer the slices between paper. Stacked slices weld together and tear when you separate them.
3. Take it out of the fridge 30 minutes before serving. Cold ham has almost no aroma, and the fat stays waxy instead of melting.
4. Lay the slices loosely on a plate in soft folds. Flat overlapping slices look tidy and eat worse.
5. Add the burrata, torn open, and the fruit cut into wedges or halves.
6. Drizzle olive oil over the cheese only, not the ham. The ham has enough fat of its own.
7. Crack pepper over, scatter the rocket, and serve with grissini. No vinegar, no balsamic glaze.

**Cook's note:** If you're wrapping melon, do it at the last second. The fruit's moisture makes the ham slippery and dulls the salt within a few minutes.

## 3. Tajarin al Tartufo Bianco

Piedmontese egg noodles, cut thin as thread, with a lot of yolks and very little else. White truffle season runs October to December and the truffle is never cooked, only shaved.

**Serves 4 | Prep 45 min plus resting | Cook 3 min**

**Ingredients**
- 300 g Italian 00 flour
- 20 egg yolks (yes, twenty)
- 1 tbsp olive oil
- Pinch of salt

*To finish*
- 120 g unsalted butter
- 4 tbsp pasta water
- 30 g Parmigiano Reggiano, grated fine
- 20 to 30 g fresh white truffle
- Salt

**Method**

1. Mound the flour, make a well, and add the yolks, oil, and salt. Work them in with a fork, drawing flour from the inside wall.
2. Knead 10 minutes until smooth and quite firm. It'll feel stiffer than normal pasta dough because of the yolk ratio. Wrap and rest 1 hour.
3. Roll in a machine down to the second-thinnest setting, or by hand until nearly translucent.
4. Flour the sheet well, fold it loosely, and cut into strands 1 to 2 mm wide. Toss with semolina and leave in loose nests.
5. Melt the butter gently in a wide pan until foaming but not coloured.
6. Boil the tajarin in well-salted water for 60 to 90 seconds. Lift straight into the butter with tongs, add pasta water, and toss hard for 30 seconds so it emulsifies.
7. Take off the heat, add the cheese, and toss again. Plate, then shave the truffle over at the table so the smell hits everyone at once.

**Cook's note:** Buy truffle by weight the day you're cooking. It loses aroma fast, and a three-day-old white truffle is an expensive disappointment.

## 4. Pasta 'ncasciata

Sicilian baked pasta with aubergine, meat, egg, and caciocavallo. Rich and structured enough to slice, and Montalbano's favourite dish, if you follow the books.

**Serves 6 | Prep 40 min | Cook 1 hr**

**Ingredients**
- 500 g rigatoni or anelletti
- 2 aubergines, sliced 1 cm thick
- 400 g minced beef or a mix of beef and pork
- 1 onion, 2 garlic cloves, chopped
- 700 ml tomato passata
- 150 ml red wine
- 3 hard-boiled eggs, sliced
- 150 g caciocavallo or provola, cubed
- 100 g salami, diced
- 80 g Pecorino, grated
- Basil, olive oil, breadcrumbs
- Salt, pepper

**Method**

1. Salt the aubergine slices and leave 30 minutes, then rinse and dry. Fry in olive oil until golden on both sides and drain on paper.
2. Soften the onion and garlic in oil, add the mince, and brown it hard.
3. Add the wine and reduce, then the passata. Simmer 40 minutes until thick and dark. Season and tear in basil.
4. Boil the pasta 3 minutes short of al dente. It finishes in the oven.
5. Toss the drained pasta with two thirds of the sauce and half the Pecorino.
6. Oil a deep dish and line the base and sides with fried aubergine. Fill with half the pasta, then the egg slices, salami, and cubed cheese. Add the rest of the pasta, remaining sauce, and any leftover aubergine on top.
7. Cover with breadcrumbs and Pecorino and bake at 190°C for 30 minutes. Rest 15 minutes before cutting, or it collapses.

**Cook's note:** Frying the aubergine gives a better texture than roasting, but roasting at 220°C with oil uses a quarter of the oil and is a fair trade on a weeknight.

## 5. Pappardelle al Ragù di Cinghiale

Wide ribbons with a slow wild boar ragù. Pork shoulder works if boar isn't available, and the marinade is what tames the gaminess.

**Serves 6 | Prep 30 min plus overnight | Cook 3 hr**

**Ingredients**

*Pasta*
- 400 g 00 flour
- 4 eggs
- 2 yolks
- Pinch of salt

*Ragù*
- 1 kg wild boar shoulder, cubed, or pork shoulder
- 750 ml red wine, Chianti or similar
- 2 bay leaves, 6 juniper berries, 1 cinnamon stick
- 1 onion, 2 carrots, 2 celery sticks, finely diced
- 4 garlic cloves
- 400 g chopped tomatoes
- 2 tbsp tomato paste
- 300 ml stock
- Olive oil, rosemary, salt, pepper
- Parmigiano to serve

**Method**

1. Marinate the meat overnight in half the wine with the bay, juniper, and cinnamon. Drain and dry it, discarding the marinade.
2. Make the pasta: mound the flour, add eggs and yolks, knead 10 minutes, rest 1 hour wrapped.
3. Brown the meat hard in olive oil, in batches. Set aside.
4. Cook the soffritto slowly in the same pot for 15 minutes until soft and sweet.
5. Return the meat, add the remaining wine and reduce by half, then the tomatoes, paste, stock, and rosemary. Simmer, barely bubbling, for 2½ hours.
6. Shred the meat with two forks in the pot and reduce until thick. Season.
7. Roll the pasta to 1 mm and cut into 2.5 cm ribbons. Boil 2 minutes, then toss with the ragù and a splash of pasta water in the pan. Cheese at the table.

**Cook's note:** Ragù should coat the pasta, not sit in a pool. If yours is loose, boil it hard uncovered for 10 minutes before saucing.

## 6. Strudel di Mele

Alto Adige apple strudel. The pastry is a stretched dough, not puff, and thin enough to read newsprint through. The breadcrumbs are structural, not filler.

**Serves 8 | Prep 40 min plus 1 hr rest | Cook 45 min**

**Ingredients**

*Dough*
- 250 g plain flour
- 120 ml warm water
- 3 tbsp neutral oil
- 1 egg
- Pinch of salt

*Filling*
- 1 kg tart apples, peeled and sliced thin
- 100 g sugar
- 2 tsp cinnamon
- 80 g raisins soaked in 3 tbsp rum
- 60 g pine nuts or walnuts
- Zest and juice of 1 lemon
- 80 g breadcrumbs fried in 60 g butter until golden
- 50 g melted butter for brushing
- Icing sugar

**Method**

1. Knead the dough ingredients for 10 minutes until very smooth and elastic. Brush with oil, cover with a warm bowl, and rest 1 hour.
2. Toss the apples with sugar, cinnamon, lemon, raisins, and nuts. Let the mixture sit 10 minutes, then drain off the juice that collects.
3. Spread a clean tablecloth on the table and flour it. Roll the dough out, then work your floured hands underneath and stretch it outward from the centre, walking around the table.
4. Keep going until it's about 50 by 70 cm and translucent. Trim the thick edges off.
5. Brush with melted butter, scatter the fried breadcrumbs over two thirds, and pile the apples along the near edge.
6. Lift the cloth and let it roll the strudel over on itself. Tuck the ends under and slide it onto a lined tray, seam down, curving it if needed.
7. Brush with butter and bake at 190°C for 40 to 45 minutes, brushing twice more. Cool 20 minutes, dust with icing sugar, and serve with cream.

**Cook's note:** Tears happen. Patch them with a scrap of dough and keep going, since the roll hides everything.

## 7. Canederli con Formaggio

Bread dumplings from the Dolomites, made with stale bread and cheese, served either in broth or with browned butter. Peasant food that outlived its poverty.

**Serves 4 | Prep 25 min | Cook 20 min**

**Ingredients**
- 300 g stale white bread, cut into 1 cm cubes
- 250 ml warm milk
- 3 eggs
- 150 g cheese: a mix of smoked scamorza, Fontina, and a hard aged cheese
- 1 small onion, finely chopped and softened in butter
- 3 tbsp chopped parsley and chives
- 3 tbsp plain flour, plus more if needed
- Nutmeg, salt, pepper
- 1.5 litres beef broth, or 80 g butter and sage

**Method**

1. Pour the warm milk over the bread cubes and leave 20 minutes, turning once. The bread should be soft throughout and not swimming.
2. Beat the eggs with salt, pepper, and nutmeg, then mix into the bread along with the cheese, onion, and herbs.
3. Squeeze the mixture through your fingers until it holds together. Add the flour a spoonful at a time until you can shape a ball that doesn't slump.
4. Rest 15 minutes. Roll into balls the size of a small apple, about 70 g each, with damp hands.
5. Test one in barely simmering salted water for 12 minutes. If it disintegrates, add another spoon of flour to the mix.
6. Poach the rest for 15 minutes at a gentle simmer. Boiling breaks them apart.
7. Serve in hot broth, or drained and dressed with butter browned with sage leaves and a shower of grated cheese.

**Cook's note:** Fresh bread will not work. It turns to paste. If your bread isn't stale, dry the cubes in a low oven for 20 minutes first.

## 8. Risotto alla Trevigiana

Risotto with radicchio di Treviso, which turns the rice a strange purple-grey and tastes pleasantly bitter against the wine and cheese.

**Serves 4 | Prep 15 min | Cook 30 min**

**Ingredients**
- 320 g Carnaroli rice
- 2 heads radicchio di Treviso, shredded
- 1 small onion or 2 shallots, very finely chopped
- 1.2 litres chicken or vegetable stock, kept hot
- 150 ml dry red wine, or Prosecco
- 60 g butter, half cold and cubed
- 3 tbsp olive oil
- 60 g Parmigiano or Grana, grated
- Salt, black pepper
- 100 g Gorgonzola, optional

**Method**

1. Sweat the onion in the oil and half the butter over low heat for 8 minutes without colouring.
2. Add two thirds of the radicchio and cook 5 minutes until wilted and soft.
3. Add the rice and toast it for 2 minutes, stirring, until the grains look chalky at the edges and translucent at the centre.
4. Pour in the wine and stir until it's fully absorbed.
5. Add hot stock one ladle at a time, stirring often, waiting until each addition is nearly absorbed before the next. About 17 minutes total.
6. Add the remaining raw radicchio in the last 3 minutes so some of it stays sharp.
7. Take off the heat, beat in the cold butter and cheese hard for a full minute, then rest covered 2 minutes. It should ripple when you shake the pan.

**Cook's note:** That final vigorous beating is called mantecatura and it's what makes risotto creamy. Do it off the heat, with cold butter, and don't be gentle.

## 9. Tiramisu

Coffee, mascarpone, eggs, savoiardi. The classic version uses raw eggs, which many people would rather avoid, so this one cooks the yolks into a zabaglione instead.

**Serves 8 | Prep 30 min plus 6 hr chilling**

**Ingredients**
- 5 egg yolks
- 100 g caster sugar
- 60 ml Marsala or dark rum
- 500 g mascarpone, room temperature
- 250 ml heavy cream, cold
- 300 ml strong espresso, cooled
- 2 tbsp coffee liqueur, optional
- 24 savoiardi (hard Italian ladyfingers)
- Cocoa powder for dusting

**Method**

1. Whisk the yolks, sugar, and Marsala in a bowl over simmering water for 8 to 10 minutes until thick, pale, and hot to the touch, around 70°C. Whisk constantly so it doesn't scramble.
2. Cool the zabaglione over ice, whisking now and then, until it's at room temperature.
3. Beat the mascarpone briefly until smooth. Overworking makes it grainy.
4. Fold the zabaglione into the mascarpone.
5. Whip the cream to soft peaks and fold it in gently.
6. Mix the espresso with the liqueur. Dip each savoiardo for one second per side. One second. They keep absorbing after they leave the coffee.
7. Layer biscuits and cream twice in a dish, smooth the top, and chill at least 6 hours. Dust with cocoa immediately before serving, never before.

**Cook's note:** Use hard savoiardi, not soft sponge fingers. Soft ones turn to sludge instantly and you'll get a coffee-flavoured puddle.

## 10. Guanciale

Cured pork jowl, and the correct fat for carbonara, amatriciana, and gricia. It takes three weeks and almost no effort, and it costs a fraction of buying it.

**Makes about 900 g | Prep 30 min | Cure 3 weeks**

**Ingredients**
- 1.2 kg pork jowl, skin on, glands trimmed out
- 35 g fine sea salt (about 3% of the meat weight)
- 3 g curing salt #2, optional but sensible for a long cure
- 20 g coarse black pepper, plus more for coating
- 15 g sugar
- 4 garlic cloves, crushed
- 3 sprigs rosemary, chopped
- 2 tsp fennel seed, crushed
- 3 bay leaves, crumbled

**Method**

1. Trim the jowl into a neat rectangle and remove any glands, which look like pale firm lumps. They taste bad.
2. Weigh the trimmed meat and recalculate the salt at 3% if it's changed much.
3. Mix all the cure ingredients and rub them into every surface, pressing hard.
4. Put it in a bag or non-reactive container, cover, and refrigerate 7 days, turning daily and redistributing the liquid that draws out.
5. Rinse thoroughly, pat dry, and coat the meat side heavily in fresh cracked pepper.
6. Pierce a corner, tie with string, and hang somewhere cool and humid: 12 to 15°C at 65 to 75% humidity. A cellar, a wine fridge, or a spare fridge with a bowl of water in it.
7. It's ready when it has lost about 30% of its post-cure weight, usually 2 to 3 weeks. Firm all the way through with no soft spots. Wrap and keep refrigerated for months.

**Cook's note:** White powdery mould is normal and harmless. Wipe it with vinegar. Green, black, or fuzzy mould means throw it out, no debate.

## 11. Panzerotti

Fried filled dough from Puglia. Half-moon shaped, tomato and mozzarella inside, and hot enough on the first bite to be a genuine hazard.

**Makes 12 | Prep 30 min plus 3 hr rising | Cook 20 min**

**Ingredients**

*Dough*
- 500 g 00 flour
- 300 ml warm water
- 7 g instant yeast
- 10 g salt
- 2 tbsp olive oil
- 1 tsp sugar

*Filling*
- 300 g mozzarella, diced and drained overnight
- 250 g chopped tomatoes, drained well
- 1 tsp dried oregano
- Salt, pepper, olive oil
- Optional: anchovy, capers, or diced cooked ham
- Oil for deep frying

**Method**

1. Mix the dough and knead 8 minutes until smooth. Rise 2 to 3 hours until doubled.
2. Drain both the mozzarella and the tomatoes properly. Wet filling causes bursting, and bursting in hot oil is genuinely dangerous.
3. Season the tomatoes with oregano, salt, pepper, and a little oil.
4. Divide the dough into 12 pieces, roll each into a 14 cm circle about 3 mm thick.
5. Put a spoonful of tomato and a few cubes of cheese slightly off-centre. Don't overfill, leave a clear 2 cm border.
6. Fold over, press the air out from the filling outward, and seal by pressing with a fork or by folding and crimping the edge.
7. Fry in oil at 175°C for 2 to 3 minutes a side until deep golden and puffed. Drain on a rack and wait five minutes before eating.

**Cook's note:** Any panzerotto with a weak seal will open in the pan. Check each one before it goes in, and fry the suspect ones last.

## 12. Erbazzone Reggiano

A flat savoury pie from Reggio Emilia, filled with chard, Parmigiano, and lardo or pancetta. Thin crust, thick filling, and it's just as good cold.

**Serves 8 | Prep 40 min | Cook 45 min**

**Ingredients**

*Pastry*
- 350 g plain flour
- 80 g lard or 90 g olive oil
- 130 ml warm water
- 1 tsp salt

*Filling*
- 1.2 kg Swiss chard or spinach, stalks removed
- 150 g pancetta or lardo, diced
- 1 onion, 4 spring onions, chopped
- 3 garlic cloves
- 150 g Parmigiano Reggiano, grated
- 2 eggs
- 3 tbsp breadcrumbs
- Nutmeg, salt, pepper
- A little lard or oil for the top

**Method**

1. Work the flour, lard, salt, and water into a soft dough. Knead 5 minutes, wrap, and rest 30 minutes.
2. Boil the chard 3 minutes, drain, cool, then squeeze it dry with real force. Chop it fine.
3. Render the pancetta, then add the onions, spring onions, and garlic and cook 8 minutes until soft.
4. Mix the chard with the onion mixture, cheese, eggs, breadcrumbs, and plenty of nutmeg and pepper. Season carefully since the pancetta and cheese are both salty.
5. Roll two thirds of the dough very thin and line a 28 cm tin, letting it overhang.
6. Spread the filling flat, roll the remaining dough into a thin lid, seal the edges, and trim.
7. Prick all over with a fork, rub the top with lard, and bake at 200°C for 40 to 45 minutes until browned and crisp. Cool 20 minutes before cutting.

**Cook's note:** Squeeze the greens harder than you think necessary. A cup of water left in the chard will make the bottom crust soggy every time.

## 13. Crespelle alla Valdostana

Crêpes rolled around ham and Fontina, baked in béchamel. From Valle d'Aosta, and Fontina is the one ingredient that doesn't substitute well.

**Serves 4 | Prep 30 min | Cook 30 min**

**Ingredients**

*Crêpes*
- 130 g plain flour
- 3 eggs
- 350 ml milk
- 30 g melted butter
- Pinch of salt

*Béchamel*
- 60 g butter, 60 g flour, 700 ml milk
- Nutmeg, salt, white pepper

*Filling*
- 300 g Fontina d'Aosta, sliced thin
- 200 g cooked ham, sliced
- 60 g Parmigiano, grated
- 30 g butter

**Method**

1. Whisk the crêpe ingredients into a smooth thin batter and rest 30 minutes.
2. Cook 12 thin crêpes in a lightly buttered 20 cm pan, about a minute a side. Stack them and set aside.
3. For the béchamel, melt the butter, whisk in the flour, and cook 2 minutes. Add the milk gradually, whisking, and simmer until it coats a spoon. Season with nutmeg.
4. Lay a slice of ham and a few slices of Fontina on each crêpe, add a spoonful of béchamel, and roll into a cylinder.
5. Spread a layer of béchamel in a buttered baking dish.
6. Arrange the rolls seam side down, pour the rest of the béchamel over, and scatter the Parmigiano and dots of butter.
7. Bake at 200°C for 20 minutes, then grill for 3 minutes until the top blisters. Rest 5 minutes.

**Cook's note:** Fontina releases a lot of oil when it melts. That's normal and it's the best part, so don't try to blot it.

## 14. Arrosticini

Abruzzese sheep skewers. Small cubes of mutton on thin wooden sticks, grilled fast over a long narrow brazier. Salt is the only seasoning and that's not an omission.

**Serves 4 | Prep 40 min | Cook 6 min**

**Ingredients**
- 1 kg mutton or hogget leg and shoulder, ideally with some fat
- 100 g sheep fat, if your meat is lean
- 40 thin wooden skewers, 25 cm
- Coarse sea salt
- Olive oil, optional
- Bread and red wine to serve

**Method**

1. Chill the meat until very firm, almost frozen at the edges. It cuts far more cleanly.
2. Cut into cubes of about 1.5 cm. Small and even. This is the whole technique.
3. Thread 8 to 10 cubes onto each skewer, alternating a piece of fat every third cube. Push them together tightly so they protect each other from the heat.
4. Trim the ends flat with scissors so the skewers sit evenly.
5. Build a charcoal fire and rake it into a narrow bed. Traditionally the skewers rest across a channel so only the meat sits over the coals and the sticks stay cool.
6. Grill 2 to 3 minutes a side over high heat, turning once. Salt them as they come off.
7. Serve immediately on a pile, with bread rubbed with olive oil and warmed on the grill. Eat with your hands, straight from the skewer.

**Cook's note:** Resist marinating them. Arrosticini are about sheep and fire, and anything else you add is just covering up the meat.

## 15. Mezzelune

Half-moon filled pasta, most often ricotta and spinach in the north, dressed with butter and sage. Bigger than ravioli, quicker to make, harder to overfill.

**Serves 4 | Prep 50 min | Cook 4 min**

**Ingredients**

*Pasta*
- 300 g 00 flour
- 3 eggs
- 1 yolk
- Pinch of salt

*Filling*
- 400 g ricotta, drained overnight
- 250 g spinach, cooked and squeezed bone dry
- 80 g Parmigiano, grated
- 1 egg yolk
- Nutmeg, salt, pepper
- Zest of ½ lemon

*Sauce*
- 100 g butter
- 12 sage leaves
- 40 g Parmigiano
- Toasted pine nuts, optional

**Method**

1. Make the dough, knead 10 minutes, and rest wrapped for 45 minutes.
2. Chop the squeezed spinach finely and mix with ricotta, Parmigiano, yolk, zest, nutmeg, salt, and pepper. Taste it. It should be seasoned assertively, because pasta dilutes everything.
3. Roll the dough to about 1 mm, working with one piece at a time and keeping the rest covered.
4. Cut 8 cm circles with a cutter or glass.
5. Pipe or spoon a heaped teaspoon into the centre of each. Brush the edge with water only if the dough has dried out.
6. Fold over and seal, pressing outward from the filling to push all the air out. Trapped air makes them burst.
7. Boil in well-salted water 3 to 4 minutes. Meanwhile brown the butter with the sage until nutty. Lift the pasta into the pan with a slotted spoon, add a splash of pasta water, toss, and finish with cheese and pine nuts.

**Cook's note:** Drain the ricotta overnight in a sieve. Undrained ricotta makes a wet filling that soaks through the pasta while it waits on the tray.
