# Indonesian Food

Fifteen dishes. Most of them start the same way: pound or blend a wet spice paste, fry it in oil until it stops smelling raw, then build the dish on top. That frying step is called *menumis*, and rushing it is the single most common reason home-cooked Indonesian food tastes flat. Give the paste five to eight minutes. You'll know it's ready when the oil separates and floats to the edges.

---

## 1. Soto Betawi

Jakarta's beef soup, made rich with coconut milk and, traditionally, fresh cow's milk. The dairy is what separates it from every other soto in the country. It's heavy in the best way.

**Serves 6 | Prep 30 min | Cook 2 hr**

**Ingredients**

*Broth*
- 800 g beef brisket or shin, in 3 cm cubes
- 300 g beef tripe or lung, optional but traditional
- 2.5 litres water
- 3 stalks lemongrass, bruised
- 5 kaffir lime leaves
- 3 salam leaves (Indonesian bay)
- 5 cm galangal, sliced
- 2 cinnamon sticks
- 4 cloves
- 400 ml coconut milk
- 250 ml full-fat milk
- 2 tsp salt, plus more to taste

*Spice paste*
- 10 shallots
- 6 garlic cloves
- 5 candlenuts, toasted
- 3 cm ginger
- 2 tsp ground coriander
- 1 tsp white pepper
- ½ tsp ground cumin
- ½ tsp turmeric powder
- 3 tbsp neutral oil

*To serve*
- Fried shallots, diced tomato, spring onion, emping crackers, lime wedges, sambal

**Method**

1. Cover the beef with the water, bring to a boil, and skim the grey foam off the top for the first ten minutes. Skimming properly is what gives you a clear-tasting broth.
2. Add lemongrass, lime leaves, salam, galangal, cinnamon, and cloves. Simmer gently, lid ajar, for about 90 minutes until the beef is tender.
3. Meanwhile blend the paste ingredients into a smooth purée with a splash of water. Fry in the oil over medium heat for 6 to 8 minutes until the oil separates.
4. Scrape the fried paste into the pot. Simmer 20 minutes more.
5. Stir in the coconut milk and fresh milk. Keep the heat low from here. Boiling hard will split it.
6. Season with salt. Taste. It should be savoury and slightly sweet, never spicy on its own.
7. Ladle over the garnishes in each bowl and serve with white rice and sambal on the side.

**Cook's note:** If tripe makes you nervous, boil it separately with a slice of ginger for 45 minutes first, then drain and add it to the pot. That handles the smell.

## 2. Bakso

Indonesian meatball soup. The texture is the whole point: bouncy, almost springy, nothing like an Italian meatball. You get there with very cold meat, ice, and a lot of blending.

**Serves 5 | Prep 40 min plus chilling | Cook 30 min**

**Ingredients**

*Meatballs*
- 500 g beef topside, trimmed of sinew, cut in cubes and frozen 30 minutes
- 100 g tapioca starch
- 100 g ice cubes
- 4 garlic cloves
- 1½ tsp salt
- 1 tsp white pepper
- 1 tsp baking powder
- 1 egg white

*Broth*
- 1.5 litres beef stock or bone broth
- 4 garlic cloves, fried whole then crushed
- 1 tsp white pepper
- Salt to taste

*To serve*
- Yellow noodles, rice vermicelli, blanched bok choy, celery leaves, fried shallots, sambal, sweet soy sauce

**Method**

1. Blend the half-frozen beef with garlic, salt, pepper, and the ice until it becomes a smooth, pale, sticky paste. Work in batches so the machine doesn't heat up.
2. Add the tapioca starch, baking powder, and egg white. Blend just until combined.
3. Chill the mixture 20 minutes. Bring a wide pot of water to a bare simmer, around 80°C.
4. Squeeze the paste between your thumb and forefinger to form balls, scooping each one off with an oiled spoon straight into the water.
5. They sink first, then float. Once they float, give them two more minutes, then lift out.
6. Heat the stock with the crushed fried garlic and pepper. Season well.
7. Build each bowl with noodles, greens, meatballs, then hot broth. Garnish at the table.

**Cook's note:** If your meatballs come out crumbly, the mix got warm. Everything should stay near freezing until it hits the water.

## 3. Coto Makassar

From South Sulawesi, and unlike most Indonesian soups it's thickened with ground peanuts and toasted rice rather than coconut milk. Traditionally it uses every part of the cow. You can keep it to brisket and nobody will arrest you.

**Serves 6 | Prep 30 min | Cook 2½ hr**

**Ingredients**
- 1 kg beef brisket, plus offal if you want it
- 2.5 litres water, ideally including rice washing water
- 100 g raw peanuts, fried and ground
- 3 tbsp raw rice, toasted dry until golden, then ground
- 4 tbsp neutral oil
- 4 salam leaves
- 4 cm galangal, bruised
- 2 stalks lemongrass, bruised

*Spice paste*
- 10 shallots
- 8 garlic cloves
- 5 candlenuts, toasted
- 2 tsp ground coriander
- 1 tsp cumin
- 1 tsp white pepper
- ½ tsp ground cloves

*To serve*
- Fried shallots, spring onion, lime, tauco or fermented soybean sambal, ketupat rice cakes

**Method**

1. Boil the beef in the water with salam, galangal, and lemongrass until tender, about 2 hours. Skim as it comes up to the boil.
2. Lift out the meat, slice it, and keep the broth. Strain it if you want it neater.
3. Blend the paste, then fry it in oil for 7 minutes until fragrant and the oil separates.
4. Stir the ground peanuts and ground toasted rice into the paste, then add a ladle of broth to loosen it.
5. Pour the whole lot back into the broth pot. Simmer 30 minutes so it thickens and the flavours settle.
6. Return the sliced beef. Season with salt.
7. Serve with the sambal tauco stirred in to taste. That fermented hit is what makes it Makassar.

**Cook's note:** Cooking the beef in the cloudy water left over from rinsing rice is a real technique here, not superstition. It softens the meat and adds body.

## 4. Nasi Goreng Jawa

Javanese fried rice, distinguished from the more common version by sweet soy sauce and a pounded chilli paste with shrimp paste in it. Darker, sweeter, and better.

**Serves 2 | Prep 15 min | Cook 10 min**

**Ingredients**
- 3 cups cold cooked rice, at least a day old
- 150 g chicken thigh, diced small
- 3 tbsp neutral oil
- 2 eggs
- 3 tbsp kecap manis
- 1 tsp salt
- ½ tsp white pepper
- Handful of shredded cabbage and sliced mustard greens
- 2 spring onions, sliced

*Paste*
- 6 shallots
- 4 garlic cloves
- 5 red bird's eye chillies, more if you like
- 1 tsp terasi (shrimp paste), toasted

*To serve*
- Fried egg, prawn crackers, sliced cucumber and tomato, fried shallots

**Method**

1. Pound or blend the shallots, garlic, chillies, and terasi to a rough paste.
2. Heat the oil in a wok over high heat. Fry the paste 3 minutes until it darkens.
3. Push it aside, crack in the eggs, and scramble them roughly in the same pan.
4. Add the chicken. Cook until it's no longer pink.
5. Toss in the cabbage and greens for 30 seconds, then the rice. Break up every clump with the back of your spatula.
6. Pour the kecap manis around the edge of the wok, not into the middle. It caramelises on the hot metal on the way in.
7. Season, toss hard for 2 minutes, finish with spring onion, and get it onto plates while it's still steaming.

**Cook's note:** Day-old rice is not optional. Fresh rice steams instead of frying and you end up with porridge.

## 5. Nasi Padang (Rendang and Sambal Ijo)

Nasi Padang is a spread, not a single dish: rice with a crowd of Minangkabau side dishes. Here are the two that anchor any table. Rendang takes four hours and rewards every one of them.

**Serves 6 | Prep 30 min | Cook 4 hr**

**Ingredients**

*Rendang*
- 1.2 kg beef chuck, in large 4 cm cubes
- 1.5 litres coconut milk (thick, from about 3 fresh coconuts or 4 cans)
- 3 stalks lemongrass, bruised
- 6 kaffir lime leaves
- 3 turmeric leaves if you can find them
- 2 salam leaves
- 5 cm galangal, bruised
- 2 tsp salt
- 1 tbsp palm sugar

*Rendang paste*
- 15 dried red chillies, soaked
- 12 shallots
- 8 garlic cloves
- 5 cm ginger
- 5 cm fresh turmeric
- 5 candlenuts
- 1 tsp coriander seed, ground

*Sambal ijo*
- 250 g green bird's eye and long green chillies
- 6 green tomatoes
- 6 shallots
- 3 garlic cloves
- 4 tbsp oil, salt, squeeze of lime

**Method**

1. Blend the rendang paste until completely smooth. Lumpy paste means lumpy sauce.
2. Put the paste, coconut milk, aromatics, and beef into a wide heavy pot. Do not cover it.
3. Bring to a gentle boil, then reduce to a steady simmer. Stir every 15 minutes or so at first.
4. After about 2 hours the liquid turns pale gold and thickens. This stage is kalio, and some people stop here. It's good, but keep going.
5. Over the next 90 minutes to 2 hours the coconut oil separates out and the sauce goes from creamy to dark brown to almost dry. Stir constantly through this last stretch or it will catch.
6. It's done when the meat is coated in dark, oily, almost crumbly paste and there's no free liquid. Season.
7. For the sambal ijo, steam or boil the chillies, tomatoes, shallots, and garlic for 5 minutes, then pound coarsely and stir-fry in oil for 5 minutes. Salt, lime, done.
8. Serve both with plenty of rice, plus whatever else you feel like adding.

**Cook's note:** Widen the pot, don't deepen it. Evaporation is doing the work, and a narrow pot takes twice as long.

## 6. Rawon

East Javanese black beef soup. The colour comes from keluak, a fermented nut that tastes faintly of truffle and dark chocolate. Nothing else does what it does.

**Serves 6 | Prep 30 min | Cook 2 hr**

**Ingredients**
- 800 g beef shin or brisket, cubed
- 2 litres water
- 6 keluak nuts
- 3 stalks lemongrass, bruised
- 4 kaffir lime leaves
- 3 salam leaves
- 4 cm galangal, bruised
- 3 tbsp oil
- Salt and a little palm sugar

*Spice paste*
- 8 shallots
- 6 garlic cloves
- 4 candlenuts
- 3 cm fresh turmeric
- 2 red chillies
- 1 tsp coriander seed, ground

*To serve*
- Short bean sprouts, salted duck egg, fried shallots, sambal terasi, lime

**Method**

1. Crack the keluak shells, scoop out the black paste, and soak it in warm water for 15 minutes. Discard any that smell sharp or bitter rather than earthy.
2. Boil the beef in the water until tender, roughly 90 minutes, skimming early.
3. Blend the spice paste with the soaked keluak until smooth.
4. Fry the paste in oil with the lemongrass, lime leaves, salam, and galangal for 8 minutes.
5. Tip it into the broth. Simmer 30 minutes so the colour and flavour spread through.
6. Season with salt and a pinch of palm sugar to round it off.
7. Serve over rice with raw bean sprouts on top. The crunch against the dark broth is the best part.

**Cook's note:** Buy keluak already cracked and cleaned if your shop sells it that way. The whole nuts are genuinely hard to open without a hammer.

## 7. Gado Gado

Blanched vegetables under peanut sauce. The sauce is where all the attention goes, and it should be thick enough to coat but loose enough to pour.

**Serves 4 | Prep 25 min | Cook 20 min**

**Ingredients**

*Sauce*
- 250 g raw peanuts, fried in oil until golden, then cooled
- 4 red chillies
- 3 garlic cloves
- 1 tsp toasted terasi
- 80 g palm sugar
- 2 tbsp tamarind paste
- 1½ tsp salt
- 300 ml hot water, more as needed

*Vegetables and extras*
- 200 g green beans, cut in lengths
- 200 g cabbage, shredded
- 150 g bean sprouts
- 200 g spinach or water spinach
- 2 potatoes, boiled and sliced
- 200 g firm tofu and 150 g tempeh, both fried
- 4 boiled eggs
- 1 cucumber, sliced
- Prawn crackers and fried shallots

**Method**

1. Grind the fried peanuts with the chillies, garlic, and terasi. A mortar gives better texture than a blender, but use what you have.
2. Add palm sugar, tamarind, and salt. Grind again.
3. Loosen with hot water a splash at a time until it pours slowly off a spoon. Taste and balance: it wants to be nutty, sweet, sour, and salty all at once.
4. Blanch each vegetable separately in salted water. Beans need 3 minutes, cabbage 2, spinach and sprouts about 30 seconds. Drain well.
5. Arrange everything on a platter with the tofu, tempeh, potato, egg, and cucumber.
6. Pour the sauce over just before serving, or serve it alongside so nothing goes soggy.
7. Finish with crackers and fried shallots.

**Cook's note:** Frying raw peanuts yourself beats using roasted ones from a jar by a wide margin. Cool them completely before grinding or the sauce turns oily.

## 8. Pempek Palembang

Fish and sago cakes served in a sour, sweet, garlicky vinegar sauce called cuko. The sauce is aggressive and it should be.

**Serves 5 | Prep 45 min | Cook 30 min**

**Ingredients**

*Pempek*
- 500 g Spanish mackerel fillet, skinned and boned, minced
- 250 ml ice water
- 2 tsp salt
- 1 tsp sugar
- 4 garlic cloves, grated
- 350 g sago or tapioca flour
- 4 eggs, for the stuffed kapal selam version

*Cuko*
- 250 g palm sugar
- 500 ml water
- 100 ml tamarind water
- 10 garlic cloves
- 20 bird's eye chillies
- 2 tbsp dried shrimp, optional
- 2 tsp salt, 3 tbsp vinegar

**Method**

1. Mix the minced fish with the ice water, salt, sugar, and garlic until it looks like a smooth wet paste.
2. Fold in the flour gradually with your hands. Stop as soon as it holds together. Kneading it develops toughness.
3. For kapal selam, shape a portion into a thick cup, drop in a whole raw egg, and seal the edges carefully.
4. Simmer the shaped pempek in salted water until they float, then 5 minutes more. Lift out and cool.
5. For the cuko, boil the palm sugar with the water until dissolved. Pound the garlic and chillies, add them with the tamarind and dried shrimp, and simmer 15 minutes. Strain if you like it smooth. Add vinegar and salt off the heat.
6. Deep fry the boiled pempek in hot oil until the outside blisters and turns golden.
7. Cut into pieces, drown in cuko, and top with diced cucumber and yellow noodles.

**Cook's note:** Boiled pempek keeps in the fridge for three days and freezes for a month. Fry it fresh each time.

## 9. Batagor

Bandung street food: fried fish dumplings and tofu under peanut sauce. The name is a contraction of bakso tahu goreng, which tells you exactly what's in it.

**Serves 4 | Prep 30 min | Cook 25 min**

**Ingredients**

*Dumplings*
- 300 g mackerel or tenggiri fillet, minced
- 150 g prawn meat, minced
- 120 g tapioca flour
- 1 egg
- 4 garlic cloves, grated
- 2 spring onions, finely sliced
- 1 tsp salt, ½ tsp white pepper, ½ tsp sugar
- 20 wonton wrappers
- 6 squares firm tofu, halved into pockets
- Oil for deep frying

*Peanut sauce*
- 200 g fried peanuts, ground
- 3 garlic cloves
- 4 red chillies
- 60 g palm sugar
- 1 tbsp tamarind paste
- 250 ml hot water, salt

**Method**

1. Combine the fish, prawn, flour, egg, garlic, spring onion, and seasoning into a sticky paste.
2. Hollow out each tofu square slightly and pack it with filling.
3. Push a spoonful of filling into each wonton wrapper, gathering the edges up loosely so it looks like a little sack.
4. Heat the oil to 170°C. Fry the tofu and dumplings in batches for 5 to 6 minutes until deep golden and firm.
5. Grind the sauce ingredients together, then thin with hot water to a pourable consistency. Season.
6. Cut everything into bite-sized pieces on the plate.
7. Spoon over the sauce, then add sweet soy sauce, lime, and sambal.

**Cook's note:** Fry at a moderate temperature. Too hot and the wrapper browns before the fish paste inside is cooked through.

## 10. Martabak Manis

A thick sweet pancake, sliced, buttered heavily, and stuffed. Called terang bulan in some cities. The holes in the surface are the mark of a good one, and they come from resting the batter properly.

**Serves 6 | Prep 15 min plus 1 hr rest | Cook 20 min**

**Ingredients**
- 250 g plain flour
- 40 g sugar
- ½ tsp salt
- 1 tsp instant yeast
- ½ tsp baking soda
- 2 eggs
- 400 ml warm water or milk
- 1 tsp vanilla

*Filling*
- 100 g salted butter, softened
- Condensed milk
- Grated chocolate, crushed roasted peanuts, cheddar, sesame seeds, whatever you like

**Method**

1. Whisk the flour, sugar, salt, and yeast together. Beat in the eggs and half the liquid until smooth, then add the rest.
2. Cover and rest at room temperature for an hour. The batter should look bubbly on top.
3. Stir in the baking soda right before cooking. This is what makes the holes open up.
4. Heat a heavy, deep, non-stick pan over medium-low. Pour in enough batter to fill it about 2 cm deep.
5. Cook undisturbed. Holes will appear across the surface over 4 or 5 minutes. Once they've formed and the surface looks dry at the edges, sprinkle a spoonful of sugar over the top.
6. Cover and cook another 5 minutes until the centre is set.
7. Turn out, brush the entire surface generously with butter, add fillings and condensed milk, fold in half, and slice.

**Cook's note:** Low heat throughout. High heat gives you a burnt base and a raw middle, which is the classic failure here.

## 11. Nasi Uduk

Rice cooked in coconut milk with lemongrass and pandan. Jakarta breakfast food. It's simple enough that quality depends entirely on the coconut milk you use.

**Serves 4 | Prep 10 min | Cook 30 min**

**Ingredients**
- 400 g jasmine rice, rinsed until the water runs nearly clear
- 400 ml coconut milk
- 200 ml water
- 2 stalks lemongrass, bruised and knotted
- 3 salam leaves
- 2 pandan leaves, knotted
- 3 kaffir lime leaves
- 3 cm galangal, bruised
- 1½ tsp salt
- 1 tsp ground coriander

*To serve*
- Fried chicken, omelette strips, tempe orek, fried shallots, sambal kacang, cucumber

**Method**

1. Warm the coconut milk, water, aromatics, salt, and coriander in a pot. Don't let it boil hard.
2. Add the rinsed rice. Cook over medium heat, stirring now and then, until the liquid is absorbed and the rice looks pitted with steam holes.
3. Tip the rice into a steamer lined with cloth or banana leaf, aromatics and all.
4. Steam for 20 to 25 minutes until each grain is tender and separate.
5. Fluff with a fork, pull out the lemongrass and leaves, and let it sit uncovered for a minute.
6. Pile onto plates with the sides and a good spoonful of sambal.

**Cook's note:** The two-stage absorb-then-steam method is worth the extra pot. Cooking it entirely in a rice cooker gives you a stickier, heavier result.

## 12. Bebek Goreng Madura

Duck simmered in spices until tender, then deep fried hard. Served with sambal pencit or the fierce green sambal Madura is known for.

**Serves 4 | Prep 30 min | Cook 1½ hr**

**Ingredients**
- 1 whole duck, about 1.8 kg, cut into 4 or 8 pieces
- 1.5 litres water or coconut water
- 4 salam leaves, 3 lemongrass stalks, 5 cm galangal
- Oil for deep frying

*Spice paste*
- 12 shallots
- 8 garlic cloves
- 5 candlenuts
- 4 cm ginger
- 4 cm fresh turmeric
- 2 tsp coriander seed
- 1 tsp cumin
- 2 tsp salt
- 1 tsp white pepper

*Sambal*
- 15 green bird's eye chillies
- 5 shallots
- 3 garlic cloves
- 1 tsp terasi, salt, lime, 3 tbsp hot oil

**Method**

1. Rub the duck all over with salt and lime juice, leave 10 minutes, then rinse. This cuts the gaminess.
2. Blend the paste and fry it in a large pot with a little oil for 8 minutes.
3. Add the duck, aromatics, and water. Simmer, partly covered, for about 75 minutes until a fork slides into the thigh with no resistance.
4. Let the duck cool in the liquid if you have time. It'll take on more flavour.
5. Lift out and pat completely dry. Wet duck spits violently in hot oil.
6. Deep fry at 180°C for 5 to 7 minutes until the skin is dark and crisp.
7. Pound the sambal ingredients coarsely, then pour the hot oil over the top and stir. Serve with rice.

**Cook's note:** Save the simmering liquid. Reduced down and spooned over rice, it's better than most gravies.

## 13. Sate Ayam Ponorogo

Ponorogo satay uses long strips of chicken breast rather than cubes, marinated before grilling, which gives you more surface for char. The peanut sauce is thinner than the Madura version.

**Serves 4 | Prep 30 min plus 2 hr marinating | Cook 20 min**

**Ingredients**
- 700 g chicken breast, sliced into long thin strips
- 3 tbsp kecap manis
- 3 garlic cloves, grated
- 1 tbsp tamarind water
- 1 tsp ground coriander
- 1 tsp salt
- Bamboo skewers, soaked

*Peanut sauce*
- 200 g fried peanuts, ground fine
- 4 shallots and 3 garlic cloves, fried
- 4 red chillies
- 60 g palm sugar
- 2 tbsp kecap manis
- 1 tbsp tamarind paste
- 300 ml water, salt

*Basting*
- 4 tbsp kecap manis mixed with 2 tbsp oil and a squeeze of lime

**Method**

1. Toss the chicken strips with the marinade. Cover and refrigerate at least 2 hours.
2. Thread each strip lengthwise onto a skewer, weaving it so it lies flat.
3. Grind the peanuts with the fried shallots, garlic, and chillies, then simmer with the rest of the sauce ingredients for 10 minutes until it thickens slightly.
4. Get your coals properly hot and let the flames die down to glowing embers.
5. Grill the skewers 2 minutes a side, basting each time you turn them.
6. Watch the sugar. It goes from caramelised to burnt fast, so move skewers to cooler spots as needed.
7. Serve with sauce, sliced shallot, lime, and either rice or lontong.

**Cook's note:** A gas grill or a hot ridged pan works, but you lose the smoke, which is a real part of the flavour. Compensate with an extra minute over higher heat.

## 14. Sate Kambing

Goat satay. Cubes of leg or shoulder, often with a piece of fat between them, grilled fast and served with sweet soy, raw shallot, and chilli rather than peanut sauce.

**Serves 4 | Prep 20 min plus 1 hr | Cook 15 min**

**Ingredients**
- 800 g young goat leg or shoulder, in 2.5 cm cubes
- 1 ripe papaya, or 2 tbsp grated green papaya
- 3 tbsp kecap manis
- 2 garlic cloves, grated
- 1 tsp salt, ½ tsp pepper
- Skewers, soaked

*To serve*
- 6 shallots, thinly sliced
- 8 bird's eye chillies, sliced
- 2 tomatoes, diced
- 6 tbsp kecap manis
- Lime wedges

**Method**

1. Rub the goat with the grated papaya and leave 30 minutes. The enzymes tenderise it. Don't leave it longer than an hour or the surface goes mushy.
2. Rinse the papaya off, then marinate with kecap manis, garlic, salt, and pepper for another 30 minutes.
3. Thread 4 or 5 cubes per skewer, packed close together so they stay juicy.
4. Grill over very hot coals. Two minutes a side, no more. Goat toughens the moment it overcooks.
5. Mix the shallots, chillies, tomato, and kecap manis in a bowl.
6. Slide the meat off the skewers onto that mixture, or serve them alongside.
7. Squeeze lime over and eat immediately.

**Cook's note:** Young goat has a mild flavour. If yours smells strong, soak the cubes in milk for an hour before you do anything else.

## 15. Pecel Lele

Fried catfish with sambal and raw vegetables. It's the default cheap dinner on Java, sold from tarpaulin stalls every night. The sambal does most of the work.

**Serves 4 | Prep 20 min | Cook 20 min**

**Ingredients**
- 4 catfish, about 250 g each, cleaned and scored
- 3 garlic cloves, grated
- 2 cm fresh turmeric, grated
- 2 tsp salt
- 1 tbsp lime juice
- Oil for deep frying

*Sambal*
- 12 red bird's eye chillies
- 5 large red chillies
- 6 shallots
- 4 garlic cloves
- 2 tomatoes
- 1 tsp toasted terasi
- 1 tbsp palm sugar
- 1 tsp salt
- 4 tbsp oil

*To serve*
- Cucumber, cabbage leaves, Thai basil, long beans, rice

**Method**

1. Rub the fish inside and out with garlic, turmeric, salt, and lime. Rest 15 minutes.
2. Fry the sambal ingredients (except sugar and salt) in the oil over medium heat for 6 minutes until softened and blistered.
3. Pound or blend them coarsely with the sugar and salt. Leave it rough. Smooth sambal loses something.
4. Heat the frying oil to 180°C in a deep pan.
5. Pat the fish dry and lower it in carefully. Fry 6 to 8 minutes until the skin is dark and crunchy at the edges.
6. Drain on a rack, not paper, so the underside stays crisp.
7. Serve on a plate with the sambal, the raw vegetables, and hot rice. Tear the fish apart with your hands and drag it through the sambal.

**Cook's note:** Score the fish down to the bone on both sides. It cooks faster and gets crisp in the cuts, which is where the best bites are.
