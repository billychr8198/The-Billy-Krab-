# Japanese Food

Japanese home cooking runs on a small set of liquids: soy sauce, mirin, sake, and dashi. Learn the ratios and half of these recipes stop needing a recipe. A very common base is 1 part soy, 1 part mirin, 1 part sake, plus dashi to dilute. Adjust from there.

One thing worth saying early: dashi granules are fine. Purists will tell you otherwise, but a decent instant dashi beats badly made kombu-katsuo stock, and most Japanese households keep a jar in the cupboard.

## 1. Otoro Nigiri Sushi

Fatty tuna belly over seasoned rice. There's nowhere to hide with this one, so buy the best fish you can and handle it as little as possible.

**Makes 12 pieces | Prep 40 min | Cook 25 min**

**Ingredients**

*Shari (rice)*
- 300 g Japanese short-grain rice
- 330 ml water
- 5 cm piece of kombu
- 60 ml rice vinegar
- 25 g sugar
- 8 g salt

*Fish and finishing*
- 250 g otoro (tuna belly), sushi grade, well chilled
- Wasabi, real if you can get it
- Soy sauce for dipping
- Tezu: 200 ml water mixed with 1 tbsp rice vinegar

**Method**

1. Wash the rice in several changes of water until it runs mostly clear, then drain in a sieve for 20 minutes.
2. Cook with the water and kombu. Remove the kombu just before it boils. Rest 10 minutes off the heat, lid on.
3. Warm the vinegar, sugar, and salt until dissolved. Don't boil it.
4. Turn the rice into a wide bowl, pour the vinegar mix over a spatula so it spreads, and cut through the rice with slicing motions while fanning it. You want it glossy and at body temperature, never cold.
5. Trim the otoro, removing sinew. Slice against the grain at a slight angle into pieces about 8 cm by 3 cm, roughly 5 mm thick.
6. Wet your hands with tezu. Take about 18 g of rice and squeeze it lightly into an oval. Light pressure. It should hold together but collapse in the mouth.
7. Smear a little wasabi on the fish, lay the rice on top, then flip and shape with two fingers. Serve fish side down onto the plate.

**Cook's note:** Keep the tuna in the fridge until the moment you slice it, and use your longest, sharpest knife in a single pulling stroke. Sawing tears the fat.

## 2. Yokohama Style Ramen

Iekei ramen: pork bone and chicken broth with a heavy soy tare, thick straight noodles, spinach, and nori. It sits somewhere between tonkotsu and Tokyo shoyu, and it's rich enough that a small bowl goes a long way.

**Serves 4 | Prep 30 min | Cook 6 hr**

**Ingredients**

*Broth*
- 1.5 kg pork leg bones, split
- 500 g chicken carcass
- 1 onion, 1 leek, 6 garlic cloves, 5 cm ginger
- 5 litres water

*Tare*
- 200 ml dark soy sauce
- 60 ml mirin
- 60 ml sake
- 20 g dried shiitake
- 10 g katsuobushi
- 5 cm kombu

*Chashu*
- 800 g pork belly, rolled and tied
- 200 ml soy, 100 ml mirin, 100 ml sake, 50 g sugar, water to cover

*To assemble*
- 600 g thick straight ramen noodles
- Blanched spinach, sheets of nori, spring onion, chopped garlic, sesame oil, marinated soft eggs

**Method**

1. Blanch the bones in boiling water for 10 minutes, drain, and scrub off the scum. This step decides whether your broth is clean or muddy.
2. Cover with the 5 litres of fresh water, add the aromatics, and boil hard for 5 to 6 hours, topping up water as it drops. A rolling boil emulsifies the fat, which is what makes the broth opaque and creamy.
3. Braise the pork belly in the chashu liquid at a low simmer for 2 hours. Cool it in the liquid, then chill before slicing.
4. Warm all the tare ingredients to 70°C, hold 20 minutes, then strain. Don't boil it.
5. Strain the broth. Season a test bowl with about 3 tbsp tare per 350 ml broth and adjust.
6. Boil the noodles hard for the time on the packet, usually 90 seconds to 2 minutes. Drain thoroughly.
7. Tare into the bowl, hot broth over it, noodles, then chashu, spinach, egg, nori standing up at the side, spring onion, and a small pile of raw chopped garlic.

**Cook's note:** Six hours is a weekend project. A pressure cooker gets you 80 percent of the way in 90 minutes at high pressure.

## 3. Kare (Japanese Curry)

Sweeter, thicker, and gentler than its Indian ancestor. Most Japanese homes use a block of curry roux from a box, which is genuinely good, but making the roux yourself takes fifteen minutes and tastes noticeably better.

**Serves 4 | Prep 20 min | Cook 1 hr**

**Ingredients**
- 600 g beef chuck or chicken thigh, cubed
- 2 onions, sliced thin
- 2 carrots, cut in chunks
- 3 potatoes, cut in chunks
- 1 apple, grated
- 1 tbsp grated ginger
- 3 garlic cloves, grated
- 1.2 litres chicken or beef stock
- 1 tbsp soy sauce
- 1 tbsp tomato paste
- 1 tsp honey
- Oil, salt, pepper

*Roux*
- 60 g butter
- 60 g plain flour
- 3 tbsp curry powder
- 1 tsp garam masala
- ½ tsp cayenne

**Method**

1. Brown the meat hard in oil, in batches. Set aside.
2. Cook the onions in the same pot over medium-low heat for 20 minutes until deeply golden and sweet. This is the flavour base, so don't cut it short.
3. Add the garlic, ginger, and grated apple. Cook 2 minutes.
4. Return the meat, add stock, soy, tomato paste, and honey. Simmer 40 minutes.
5. Meanwhile melt the butter in a small pan, whisk in the flour, and cook over low heat for 10 minutes until it smells nutty and turns light brown. Stir in the spices off the heat.
6. Add the carrots and potatoes to the pot, simmer 15 minutes until just tender.
7. Whisk a ladle of hot broth into the roux, then stir the loosened roux into the pot. Simmer 10 minutes more until it's thick enough to hold a line when you drag a spoon through it. Serve over rice with pickled ginger.

**Cook's note:** It's better the next day, no question. The starch keeps thickening overnight, so add a splash of water when reheating.

## 4. Chicken Karaage

Marinated fried chicken with a potato starch coating. Double frying is the difference between good and very good.

**Serves 4 | Prep 15 min plus 30 min marinating | Cook 20 min**

**Ingredients**
- 800 g boneless chicken thigh, skin on, in 4 cm pieces
- 3 tbsp soy sauce
- 2 tbsp sake
- 1 tbsp mirin
- 1 tbsp grated ginger
- 2 garlic cloves, grated
- 1 tsp sesame oil
- 1 egg
- 120 g potato starch, plus more for dusting
- Neutral oil for frying
- Lemon wedges and Japanese mayonnaise

**Method**

1. Mix the chicken with soy, sake, mirin, ginger, garlic, and sesame oil. Marinate 30 minutes at room temperature, or up to overnight in the fridge.
2. Beat in the egg, then work in enough potato starch to make a loose batter clinging to each piece.
3. Roll each piece in dry potato starch just before frying and shake off the excess.
4. Heat oil to 160°C. Fry in batches for 3 minutes. The chicken should be pale gold and just cooked.
5. Rest on a rack for 4 minutes. The residual heat finishes the inside.
6. Raise the oil to 190°C and fry again for 60 to 90 seconds until deep golden and audibly crunchy.
7. Drain on a rack, salt lightly, and serve with lemon and mayo.

**Cook's note:** Potato starch, not cornstarch. Cornstarch gives a denser, sandier crust that softens quickly.

## 5. Kare Udon

Leftover curry thinned with dashi and poured over thick udon. It's a weeknight dish built for the day after curry night.

**Serves 2 | Prep 10 min | Cook 15 min**

**Ingredients**
- 400 g fresh or frozen udon noodles
- 500 ml dashi
- 300 ml Japanese curry (from recipe 3, or 3 blocks of roux)
- 2 tbsp soy sauce
- 1 tbsp mirin
- 150 g thinly sliced pork belly or beef
- 1 onion, sliced
- 1 tbsp potato starch mixed with 2 tbsp water
- 2 spring onions
- Shichimi togarashi

**Method**

1. Simmer the onion in the dashi for 5 minutes until soft and translucent.
2. Add the sliced pork, separating the pieces as they go in, and cook 3 minutes.
3. Stir in the curry, soy, and mirin. Simmer 5 minutes so it comes together.
4. Boil the udon separately according to the packet, usually 1 to 3 minutes for frozen. Drain.
5. Thicken the curry broth with the starch slurry. It should coat the noodles rather than run off them.
6. Divide the udon between deep bowls and ladle the curry over.
7. Top with spring onion and a heavy shake of shichimi.

**Cook's note:** Thickening matters more here than in curry rice. Thin curry slides straight off udon and pools at the bottom of the bowl.

## 6. Japanese Cheesecake

Light, wobbly, and barely sweet. It's a soufflé pretending to be a cheesecake, and it lives or dies on your meringue.

**Serves 8 | Prep 30 min | Cook 70 min**

**Ingredients**
- 250 g cream cheese, room temperature
- 60 g unsalted butter
- 100 ml whole milk
- 6 eggs, separated
- 60 g cake flour
- 20 g cornstarch
- 130 g caster sugar
- 1 tbsp lemon juice
- ¼ tsp salt

**Method**

1. Line an 18 cm round tin with baking paper, sides taller than the rim. Wrap the outside in foil if it isn't watertight.
2. Melt the cream cheese, butter, and milk together over a pan of simmering water until completely smooth. Cool slightly.
3. Whisk in the yolks one at a time, then sift in the flour and cornstarch and whisk until no lumps remain. Add lemon juice and salt.
4. Whip the whites, adding the sugar in three additions, to soft peaks. The peak should flop over when you lift the whisk. Stiff peaks will crack the top.
5. Fold a third of the meringue into the batter to loosen it, then fold in the rest gently in two goes.
6. Pour into the tin, tap it once on the bench, and set it in a roasting tray. Pour in hot water to come 3 cm up the sides.
7. Bake at 160°C for 25 minutes, drop to 140°C for 45 minutes, then turn the oven off and leave the door ajar for 15 minutes before removing. The slow cool-down stops it collapsing.

**Cook's note:** Some sinking is normal. Dramatic collapse means the meringue was overwhipped or you pulled it out of a hot oven too fast.

## 7. Shabu-Shabu

Thin beef swished through hot kombu broth at the table. It's less a recipe than an arrangement, which is exactly why it's good for guests.

**Serves 4 | Prep 25 min | Cook 10 min**

**Ingredients**
- 800 g beef sirloin or ribeye, sliced 1.5 mm thin (ask a butcher or buy pre-sliced)
- 2 litres water
- 20 cm kombu
- 2 tbsp sake

*Vegetables*
- ½ napa cabbage, 1 bunch shungiku or spinach, 200 g shiitake, 1 packet enoki, 1 leek, 300 g firm tofu, 200 g udon or shirataki

*Ponzu*
- 100 ml soy sauce, 80 ml citrus juice (yuzu, or lemon and orange mixed), 50 ml mirin warmed to burn off the alcohol, 5 g katsuobushi, steeped and strained

*Goma dare*
- 4 tbsp sesame paste, 2 tbsp soy, 1 tbsp mirin, 1 tbsp rice vinegar, 1 tsp sugar, dashi to loosen

**Method**

1. Soak the kombu in the water for at least 30 minutes, ideally a few hours. Cold soaking gets more flavour with less bitterness than boiling.
2. Set the pot on a portable burner at the table. Heat until small bubbles rise around the kombu, then remove it before it boils. Add the sake.
3. Arrange the meat, vegetables, and tofu on platters.
4. Whisk both sauces and divide into small dipping bowls.
5. Start with the firmer vegetables and tofu, which need a few minutes in the broth.
6. Hold each slice of beef with chopsticks and swish it back and forth for 5 to 10 seconds. It should go from red to pink, not grey.
7. When the meat is finished, cook the noodles in the now-flavourful broth and drink what's left as soup.

**Cook's note:** Skim the foam as you go. Ten seconds with a small ladle keeps the broth clear for the end-of-meal noodles.

## 8. Yakitori

Grilled chicken skewers, either salted or glazed with tare. The tare is a sauce you build once and keep forever, dipping each batch of skewers back into it.

**Serves 4 | Prep 30 min | Cook 20 min**

**Ingredients**
- 800 g chicken thigh, in 3 cm pieces
- 6 spring onions, cut in 3 cm lengths
- Chicken skin, hearts, or livers if you want the full range
- Bamboo skewers, soaked 30 minutes
- Salt and sansho pepper

*Tare*
- 200 ml soy sauce
- 200 ml mirin
- 100 ml sake
- 60 g sugar
- 4 chicken wings or a handful of bones, roasted
- 2 garlic cloves, 3 cm ginger

**Method**

1. For the tare, roast the wings until dark, then simmer everything together for 30 minutes until reduced by about a third. Strain.
2. Thread the chicken and spring onion alternately, packing pieces tightly. Skin side should face outward on each piece.
3. Build a charcoal fire and rake the coals into a single layer. You want steady radiant heat, not flames.
4. Grill the skewers over the coals, turning every 45 seconds, for about 4 minutes.
5. For salt skewers, season before grilling and finish with sansho.
6. For tare skewers, wait until the chicken is nearly done, then dip into the tare and return to the grill. Repeat three times, letting the glaze set each time.
7. Serve immediately. Yakitori does not survive a plate journey.

**Cook's note:** Dipping before the chicken is cooked burns the sugar and leaves you with bitter, blackened skewers. Glaze at the end only.

## 9. Takoyaki

Octopus batter balls from Osaka. They're molten in the centre when done right, which is deliberate, not undercooked.

**Makes 30 | Prep 20 min | Cook 25 min**

**Ingredients**
- 200 g plain flour
- 600 ml dashi, cooled
- 2 eggs
- 1 tsp soy sauce
- ½ tsp salt
- 300 g cooked octopus, in 1.5 cm cubes
- 4 spring onions, finely sliced
- 3 tbsp tenkasu (tempura scraps)
- 2 tbsp pickled red ginger, chopped
- Neutral oil

*Toppings*
- Takoyaki sauce, Japanese mayonnaise, aonori, katsuobushi

**Method**

1. Whisk the flour, eggs, soy, salt, and dashi into a thin batter, about the consistency of single cream. Rest 30 minutes.
2. Heat a takoyaki pan over medium and oil every well generously, including the flat parts between them.
3. Fill the wells until the batter overflows across the whole surface. Overfilling is correct here.
4. Drop a piece of octopus into each well, then scatter spring onion, tenkasu, and ginger across everything.
5. After 2 minutes the edges will set. Use a skewer to cut the batter between the wells, then turn each ball 90 degrees, tucking the loose batter underneath.
6. Keep turning every minute or so for about 6 minutes total until they're round and browned all over.
7. Pile into a tray, zigzag sauce and mayo over the top, then aonori and katsuobushi. The flakes will move in the heat.

**Cook's note:** First few balls will be ugly. That's universal. Keep going and the technique clicks around number ten.

## 10. Hiroshima-Style Okonomiyaki

Layered rather than mixed, with noodles built into it. Bigger and more of an engineering exercise than the Osaka version.

**Serves 2 | Prep 20 min | Cook 25 min**

**Ingredients**
- 120 g plain flour
- 180 ml dashi or water
- 400 g cabbage, shredded very fine
- 100 g bean sprouts
- 8 slices thin pork belly
- 2 portions yakisoba or ramen noodles, cooked
- 2 eggs
- Tenkasu, spring onion, oil

*To finish*
- Okonomiyaki sauce, Japanese mayonnaise, aonori, katsuobushi

**Method**

1. Whisk the flour and dashi into a thin batter, no thicker than crêpe batter.
2. Heat a large flat griddle or non-stick pan over medium. Ladle in enough batter to make a 20 cm thin crêpe.
3. Pile the cabbage on top, mounded high. It looks like far too much. It isn't. Add sprouts, tenkasu, and spring onion.
4. Lay the pork slices over the pile, then drizzle a little batter over the pork to bind everything.
5. After 4 minutes, flip the whole stack with two spatulas in one committed motion. Press down gently and cook 6 minutes covered so the cabbage steams down.
6. On a clear section of the griddle, fry the noodles with a spoonful of sauce, shape them into a disc, then slide the cabbage stack on top.
7. Fry the eggs flat in another spot, slide the whole tower onto them, flip once more, then sauce, mayo, aonori, katsuobushi.

**Cook's note:** The flip is the scary part. Use two wide spatulas, commit fully, and accept that a bit of cabbage will escape. Push it back in.

## 11. Hamamatsu Gyoza

Hamamatsu fries its gyoza in a circle and serves them with a pile of boiled bean sprouts in the middle, which cuts the richness. More cabbage than pork in the filling, too.

**Makes 40 | Prep 45 min | Cook 20 min**

**Ingredients**
- 40 gyoza wrappers
- 400 g cabbage, finely chopped, salted and squeezed dry
- 250 g ground pork
- 1 onion, very finely chopped
- 3 garlic cloves and 2 cm ginger, grated
- 2 tbsp soy sauce
- 1 tbsp sesame oil
- 1 tsp sugar, ½ tsp white pepper
- 200 g bean sprouts, blanched
- Oil, water, and 1 tsp flour for the crust

*Dipping sauce*
- Equal soy and rice vinegar, chilli oil, a little grated garlic

**Method**

1. Salt the chopped cabbage, wait 15 minutes, then wring it out in a cloth. You'll be surprised how much water comes out. Wet filling makes gyoza burst.
2. Mix the cabbage with pork, onion, garlic, ginger, and seasonings. Stir in one direction until it turns tacky.
3. Place a teaspoon of filling on each wrapper, wet the edge, and pleat one side against the flat other side.
4. Heat oil in a non-stick pan. Arrange the gyoza in a spiral, packed close, and fry 2 minutes until the bottoms are golden.
5. Mix 100 ml water with the teaspoon of flour and pour it in. Cover immediately and steam 6 minutes.
6. Uncover and let the water cook off entirely. Another 2 minutes gives you a thin lacy crust joining them.
7. Invert onto a plate so the crisp side faces up, and heap the bean sprouts in the centre.

**Cook's note:** The flour-water slurry is what creates that crisp webbing between dumplings. Plain water just steams them.

## 12. Unagi (Kabayaki)

Grilled eel lacquered with sweet soy glaze, served over rice as unadon. Fresh eel needs butchering skill most of us don't have, so pre-prepared frozen fillets are the sensible route.

**Serves 2 | Prep 10 min | Cook 20 min**

**Ingredients**
- 2 prepared unagi fillets, about 200 g each
- 4 tbsp sake

*Tare*
- 100 ml soy sauce
- 100 ml mirin
- 50 ml sake
- 40 g sugar
- Eel bones or a piece of kombu, optional

*To serve*
- Hot short-grain rice, sansho pepper, pickled ginger

**Method**

1. Simmer the tare ingredients until reduced by a third and syrupy enough to coat a spoon, about 12 minutes. Set aside.
2. If your eel came pre-glazed, rinse the sauce off under hot water and pat dry. The packet glaze is usually too sweet.
3. Steam the fillets for 8 minutes. This is the Kanto method and it makes the flesh noticeably softer.
4. Sprinkle with sake and grill skin side down under a hot broiler for 3 minutes.
5. Brush with tare, grill 90 seconds, and repeat twice more. Watch closely, since the sugar catches fast.
6. Fill bowls with hot rice, spoon a little tare over the rice itself, then lay the eel on top.
7. Sansho pepper at the table. It's citrusy and slightly numbing, and it cuts the fat.

**Cook's note:** Don't skip the sansho. Unagi without it tastes cloying after four or five bites.

## 13. Mochi (Daifuku)

Pounded glutinous rice cake wrapped around sweet bean paste. The microwave method takes ten minutes and works genuinely well.

**Makes 10 | Prep 30 min | Cook 10 min**

**Ingredients**
- 200 g shiratamako or glutinous rice flour
- 60 g sugar
- 250 ml water
- 300 g anko (sweet red bean paste)
- Potato starch or cornstarch for dusting
- 10 small strawberries, optional but excellent

**Method**

1. Divide the anko into 10 portions and roll into balls. If using strawberries, wrap each one in anko first and chill.
2. Whisk the flour, sugar, and water in a microwave-safe bowl until completely smooth.
3. Cover with cling film and microwave 1 minute. Stir hard with a wet spatula. Microwave 1 minute more, stir again, then 30 seconds. The dough should be translucent, thick, and sticky.
4. Dust your work surface heavily with starch and tip the hot dough out. Dust the top too.
5. Let it cool just enough to handle, then divide into 10 pieces.
6. Flatten each into a disc, place a filling ball in the middle, and gather the edges up and pinch them closed. Roll seam-side down.
7. Brush off excess starch and eat the same day. Mochi hardens overnight.

**Cook's note:** Wet hands and a wet spatula are your only defence against the stickiness. Keep a bowl of water next to you.

## 14. Tamagoyaki

Rolled omelette, layer on layer. It looks simple and takes practice. The dashimaki version below has enough dashi in it to be custardy inside.

**Serves 2 | Prep 5 min | Cook 10 min**

**Ingredients**
- 4 eggs
- 4 tbsp dashi
- 1 tbsp mirin
- 1 tsp soy sauce
- 1 tsp sugar
- Pinch of salt
- Neutral oil

**Method**

1. Beat the eggs with everything else. Don't whisk in air. Cut through them with chopsticks instead, then strain through a sieve for a smooth finish.
2. Heat a rectangular tamagoyaki pan over medium-low. Oil it with a folded paper towel, keeping the towel for reuse between layers.
3. Pour in a thin layer of egg, tilting to cover the base. Pop any bubbles with your chopsticks.
4. When the surface is nearly set but still glossy, roll the egg from the far side toward you into a loose log.
5. Push the roll back to the far end, re-oil the empty pan, and pour in the next layer, lifting the roll so egg runs underneath it.
6. Repeat until the egg is gone, usually four or five layers.
7. Turn out onto a bamboo mat, shape gently while warm, then rest 5 minutes and slice into thick pieces.

**Cook's note:** Low heat throughout. Browning is considered a fault here, though a pale gold edge is nothing to worry about.

## 15. Soba

Buckwheat noodles, served cold with dipping sauce in summer or hot in broth in winter. Making them from scratch is a real skill, so I've written it honestly rather than pretending it's easy.

**Serves 4 | Prep 45 min | Cook 5 min**

**Ingredients**

*Noodles (80/20 blend)*
- 320 g buckwheat flour
- 80 g strong white flour
- 200 ml water, roughly

*Tsuyu for dipping*
- 200 ml dashi
- 50 ml soy sauce
- 50 ml mirin, warmed to cook off alcohol
- 1 tsp sugar

*To serve*
- Grated daikon, wasabi, spring onion, shredded nori

**Method**

1. Mix the flours in a wide bowl. Add water in three stages, mixing with your fingertips in a circular motion so it forms crumbs rather than a lump.
2. When it holds together when squeezed, gather and knead for 5 minutes into a smooth ball, then cone it and press out the air.
3. Roll into a large thin sheet, about 1.5 mm, dusting with buckwheat flour as you go. Buckwheat has almost no gluten, so it tears easily. Work gently.
4. Fold the sheet into layers and cut into 1.5 mm strips with a heavy sharp knife. Toss with flour to keep them separate.
5. Simmer the tsuyu ingredients briefly, then chill.
6. Boil the noodles in plenty of unsalted water for 60 to 90 seconds. Fresh soba cooks fast.
7. Drain and rinse under cold running water, rubbing gently to remove surface starch. Serve on a bamboo tray with the tsuyu and condiments alongside.

**Cook's note:** If the dough keeps cracking, raise the white flour to 30 percent. Purists prefer higher buckwheat ratios, but a noodle that holds together beats one that shatters in the pot.
